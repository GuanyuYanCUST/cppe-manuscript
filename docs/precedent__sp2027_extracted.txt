No Precedent Without Power:
Preventing Exception Ratcheting in Learning Agents
Abstract
A system may be authorized to decide an exceptional case without being authorized to turn that decision into law. Learning agents
blur this boundary in a way ordinary access control does not capture: a case-specific exception can be valid, safe, and operationally
successful; the learner can retain that success; and a later policy or retrieval path can generalize it into a reusable rule even though
no rulemaking authority was ever exercised. We call this exception ratcheting: learning-induced expansion of standing normative
authority without an authorized normative transition.
We separate three powers that must not be inferred from one another: CASE, authority to approve one bounded exception;
PRECEDENT , authority to install a reusable class of future exceptions; and AMEND , authority to widen the governing discretion
envelope itself. We give an operational definition of ratcheting through an explicit STANDING REGION , specify a labeled transition
system over normative state, and prove a normative authority confinement invariant from which no-self-ratification and root-
envelope preservation follow. We also characterize exactly when outcome-only generalization can be both sound and complete
via root-observation sufficiency: success history may inform what the learner proposes, but it cannot substitute for the root-bound
information needed to justify standing authority.
We realize this boundary in PRECEDENT GUARD , a reference-monitor architecture with typed CASE/PRECEDENT /AMEND
capabilities, proof-carrying discretion, a counterfactual challenger for necessity claims, and certified precedent refinement. The
design intentionally permits learning: successful cases may improve planning and may suggest candidate precedents. What they
cannot do is ratify themselves. We implement two structurally different containment verifiers—a monotone threshold DSL and a
non-monotone disjunctive DSL—and obtain zero unsafe installations across exhaustive adversarial generalization traces (390,625
and 15,625 histories). Across challenger-model corruption levels from 0% to 40% spurious edges, unsafe admission remains 0.0%
while conservative false rejection rises from 0% to 60.2%; under a deliberate 20× overload, the bounded challenger fails closed
rather than admitting unsupported exceptions. The resulting security principle is simple: learn how to succeed; do not learn yourself
into legislative authority.
1. Introduction
A successful exception is still only an exception. Suppose a
policy explicitly delegates authority to decide difficult cases
inside a bounded discretion envelope. A learner uses that
discretion once, the action works, and the successful trajectory
becomes part of memory, training data, or a reusable skill. The
next case is similar. The learner generalizes. After enough
successful reuse, behavior that began as a one-shot exception
can be treated as a standing rule—without any credential theft,
policy-file modification, or explicit exercise of rulemaking
power.
The security failure is subtle because every local step can
look legitimate. The original case was authorized. Learning
from it was allowed. The proposed reusable rule may even be
operationally sensible. What changed is the kind of authority:
adjudicating one fact pattern has been converted into authority
over a class of future fact patterns. That conversion is not
ordinary learning. It is a governance transition.
Learn how to succeed ̸≡ learn what is allowed.
We call the resulting failure exception ratcheting: individu-
ally valid exceptions and success feedback expand the region
that is treated as standing normative authority even though no
authorized normative transition widened the governing rule.
The phenomenon resembles normalization of deviance, where
repeated successful deviation becomes evidence that the devi-
ation is acceptable [8]; a learning system can automate that
feedback path by retaining successful cases and proposing
broader reusable policies.
1.1 The protected boundary is a separation of powers
The key distinction is not between “learning” and “no learn-
ing.” It is between three non-interchangeable powers:
• CASE: this concrete case may deviate within a bounded
scope;
• PRECEDENT : this machine-checkable class of future cases
may reuse a decision;
• AMEND : the delegated discretion envelope itself may
change.
A principal may possess the first power without the second,
and the second without the third. The paper’s core invariant is
therefore a machine-enforced separation of powers:
CASE̸⇒ PRECEDENT ,
PRECEDENT ̸⇒ AMEND.
Operational success may influence the learner’s competence
and its next proposal. It is never, by itself, proof of normative
authority.

rity definition. We therefore make standing authority explicit.

1.3 Why this is not just “ask a human”
Some deployments can synchronously escalate every diffi-
cult case to a root authority. Others intentionally delegate
local discretion because delay, availability, or operating condi-
tions make that undesirable. Our object is the latter authority
structure, not a particular application domain. Disconnected
scientific and cyber-physical systems are one forcing example,
but the same boundary appears anywhere case adjudication,
persistent reuse, and rulemaking power are deliberately sep-
arated. The mechanism must therefore preserve useful dele-
gated discretion without quietly converting it into delegated
legislation.
1.4 Design and contributions
PRECEDENT GUARD makes normative generalization an ex-
plicit security operation. A learner can propose any action
or reusable rule. A CASE certificate authorizes one context-
bound deviation; a PRECEDENT installs a reusable rule only
with a proof that its denotation remains inside the current dis-
cretion envelope; and an AMEND object is the only operation
that may widen that envelope. A counterfactual challenger can
additionally check “no lower-deviation alternative” claims for
norms that require necessity evidence. Repeated cases may
therefore make the system faster and more competent without
making it more legally permissive.
This paper makes four contributions:
• A security object for exception ratcheting. We define
STANDING REGION , give a labeled transition system over
normative state, and prove a single normative authority
confinement invariant from which no-self-ratification and
root-envelope preservation follow. We separately state
success-history noninterference as a property of norma-
tive admissibility rather than proof-cache performance.
• A limit on outcome-only ratification.We give a necessary-
and-sufficient root-observation-sufficiency characterization
of when outcome-only generalization can be both sound
and complete, strictly generalizing the earlier impossibility
argument.
• A separation-of-powers architecture. PRECEDENT -
GUARD combines typed CASE/PRECEDENT /AMEND ob-
jects, proof-carrying discretion, certified precedent refine-
ment, explicit version/key-epoch handling, and an optional
counterfactual challenger with fail-closed resource bounds.
• Claim-matched mechanism evidence. We implement two
structurally different containment verifiers—one monotone
and one non-monotone/disjunctive—and stress both under
adversarial generalization. We also report sampling sensi-
tivity, the challenger’s conservative-rejection curve under
model over-approximation, and overload behavior up to
20× nominal. These experiments test the mechanism and
its stated trade-offs; they do not claim empirical prevalence
in deployed LLM agents.
2. Background and Gap
2.1 Hard rules, defeasible norms, and normative power
Security traditionally favors precise enforcement boundaries.
Least privilege and reference monitoring aim to mediate
security-sensitive actions [9, 10]. Usage-control systems fur-
ther allow mutable attributes and continuing decisions [ 11].
Long-lived autonomy nevertheless contains rules that can-
not be encoded as absolute prohibitions without sacrificing

mission resilience.
Normative multi-agent systems provide richer concepts.
The T-Norm methodology explicitly represents obligations,
prohibitions, permissions, exemptions, exceptions, and nor-
mative powers [2]. Oren, Luck, and Miles formalize powers
to create, delete, or modify norms and even powers over nor-
mative powers [1]. These models are useful foundations for
our distinction between adjudication and rulemaking.
Our security concern begins after a valid exception is issued.
The operational learner may absorb the case into memory,
policy parameters, retrieved examples, or a reusable skill. If
future authorization logic trusts that learned representation,
a one-case exception can become a standing decision rule
without any explicit exercise of normative power.
2.2 Norm emergence and runtime revision
Norm emergence studies how populations converge on com-
mon behavior, often from bottom-up interaction, learning,
observation, or payoff structures [12]. Runtime norm-revision
work intentionally learns how norm compliance or violation
correlates with system objectives and changes sanctions ac-
cordingly [3, 4]. In these systems, emergence or revision is a
feature and the revision component is part of the governance
design.
We study the opposite trust boundary. The root norm is
already known and must not change unless an authorized rule-
making operation occurs. The learner may improve freely, but
its successful cases are not themselves a source of constitu-
tional authority.
2.3 Restraining bolts and action certificates
Restraining bolts place an authority-defined specification out-
side the learning policy [5, 6]; normative restraining bolts and
ordered variants target legal, social, and ethical norm com-
pliance and prioritized violation minimization [7, 13]. They
are complementary to our approach. A restraining layer still
needs a semantics for exceptions and for whether a learned
exception classifier is allowed to widen.
Proof-carrying authentication asks a requester to provide
evidence for authorization [14]; recent proof-carrying agent-
action work similarly binds agent actions to portable evidence
and approval semantics [15]. PRECEDENT GUARD adopts the
proof-carrying philosophy but protects a different transition:
the conversion of a case approval into reusable normative
authority.

3.4 Adversary and trusted computing base
No malicious agent is required. For a stronger guarantee, how-
ever, we allow the learner and scheduler to be adversarial: they
may choose candidate precedents, exploit successful cases,
select legal task contexts, and attempt to broaden future excep-
tions. They cannot forge cryptographic certificates, modify
root policy, bypass the PEP, or compromise the verifier.
The trusted computing base consists of the PEP, root
norm/envelope state, certificate verifier, monotonic (version,
key-epoch) pair, and the small counterfactual challenger for
norms that require a necessity check. Agent models, prompts,
memories, reward models, planners, and precedent proposals
are outside the TCB.
We now state four additional trust-boundary items that were
previously left implicit or deferred to Limitations. Each names
a threat this paper does not solve and explains why it is a
distinct security question from exception ratcheting.
(T1) Evidence integrity is out of scope, and is a different
attack. PRECEDENT GUARD guarantees that success history
cannot substitute for root authority. It does not guarantee
e = truth. Context evidence is assumed to originate from
a trusted sensor, attested estimator, or root-approved mea-
surement pipeline, independent of the learner. If the learner
can itself fabricate or biase (for example by controlling the
downstream process that produces a threshold-adjacent sen-
sor reading), that is an evidence-integrity attack (“evidence
inflation”), not exception ratcheting: it defeats the discretion
contract by corrupting its input rather than by bypassing the
CASE/PRECEDENT/ AMEND separation. Both attacks mat-
ter; only the second is this paper’s subject.
(T2) Key epochs and revocation. In deployments where
root contact may be intermittent, systems must tolerate root
key rotation and after-the-fact revocation of a previously certi-
fied precedent (e.g., a PRECEDENT is later found to rest on
a flawed containment proof, or an AMEND authority is com-
promised). We introduce a constitutional key epochκ; every
normative object carries (v,κ ) for versionv and epochκ. The
root may sign ROTATE(κ→κ+1) or REVOKE(certID,v′).
The local PEP maintains a monotonically advancing (v,κ )
and treats any object bound to an older, explicitly revoked,
or superseded pair as invalid. We are explicit about the re-
sulting limitation: a fully disconnected system cannot learn
of a revocation the instant it is issued. Freshness is bounded
by certificate expiry, a preloaded key schedule, and update
on reconnection – this is an availability/ freshness trade-off
inherent to disconnected operation, not a defect specific to
PRECEDENT GUARD .
(T3) Challenger availability. The counterfactual chal-
lenger (Section 6.4) is itself a resource that an adversar-
ial scheduler could try to exhaust by manufacturing many
challenge-requiring requests. We bound challenger compute
by a budget Bc (maximum concurrent challenges), a per-
principal rate limit, and a strict timeout, with the explicit
fail-closed rule timeout⇒ deny exception (never⇒ admit).
Section 8 measures the resulting throughput/latency/timeout
behavior under load, including a 20×-overload adversarial
flood.

by several norms simultaneously, A(q) = {N1,...,N m}.

4.3 Outcome-only ratification: a sufficiency characteriza-
tion

on operational history can both install authorized rules and
reject unauthorized ones, by exhibiting two policies that agree
on history but disagree on a proposal. That argument is correct

look at root-bound evidence, then two roots that produce
identical observations are trivially indistinguishable to it. We
now state the general condition under which any observation-

the earlier result is a corollary.

Our guarantees require: (1) registered high-consequence ac-
tions pass through the PEP; (2) root envelopes and the (v,κ )
pair are authenticated and monotonic; (3) CASE objects can-
not be replayed outside their case scope; (4) the contain-

different concrete argument (exhaustive enumeration over a
bounded domain, rather than least-favorable-point evaluation
of a monotone score). Silent bypass of the PEP or compromise
of the root key is outside scope. The learner itself is untrusted.

5.4 Success-history noninterference, corrected
The original noninterference argument stated that if one execu-
tion lacks a cached precedent, “it can construct an equivalent
case proof,” and concluded that admission is invariant to his-
tory. This blurs two different things: whether a request is

5.5 Bounded offline discretion
Proposition 1 (Offline discretion availability). If a request is
inside the current discretion envelope, satisfies its evidence
obligations, and passes any required counterfactual check,
the PEP can authorize it while the root is offline. No AMEND
is required.
We state this as a proposition rather than a theorem: it
is a capability/availability claim about what the mechanism
permits, not a confinement guarantee about what it prevents,
and grouping it with Theorem 2 and Theorem 3 previously
suggested an equivalent proof burden that it does not carry.
This matters operationally: the defense is not “call Earth.” It
preserves the exact offline discretion already delegated while
blocking only expansion beyond that delegation.
5.6 Challenger soundness
Lemma 1 (Sound no-better-alternative certificate). If chal-
lenger modelMc over-approximates all concrete plans satisfy-
ing the goal and hard invariants, and no plan inMc meets the
deadline with deviation strictly belowd, then no such concrete
plan exists.
The converse need not hold: over-approximation can create
spurious alternatives, causing conservative rejection but not
unsafe admission. Section 8 quantifies exactly this trade-off
rather than stating it only qualitatively.

8.1 Research questions
We ask whether success-only experience reuse expands a
standing exception region under a monotone DSL (RQ1) and
under a structurally different, non-monotone DSL (RQ1 ′);
whether PRECEDENT GUARD prevents expansion while still

9. Why Simpler Defenses Are Insufficient
Make every soft norm hard. This prevents ratcheting but
removes resilience and turns unforeseen conflicts into fail-
closed behavior.
Let a supervisor decide every case. A local supervisor still
needs exception semantics. If its classifier or precedent mem-
ory is learned from operational success without a separate
authority check, the ratchet moves into the supervisor. Re-
quiring root approval for every case eliminates the delegated-
discretion setting rather than solving the case-to-rule transi-
tion.
Use multiple agents or majority vote. V oting changes who
interprets the case, not what gives the interpretation rulemak-
ing authority. Shared training, memory, or success history can
also correlate the judges.
Treat prior successful exceptions as precedent. This is
the vulnerability. A prior case proves that the prior case
was admitted and worked. It does not prove that a broader
predicate was delegated.
Automatically revise the norm from runtime data. This
is valid when an explicit norm-revision component is autho-
rized [3, 4]. Our threat model is precisely the case where
the learner lacks that power. Authorized revision remains
available through AMEND.
Use a restraining bolt. Restraining bolts constrain learned
behavior [5, 7]. PRECEDENT GUARD addresses an orthogonal
transition: a defeasible norm requires exception semantics,
and the security question is whether learning may broaden the
exception predicate. A restraining-bolt implementation could
use PRECEDENT GUARD as its authority layer for exception
updates.

10. Discussion
10.1 Adjudication is not rulemaking
The useful separation is Execution ̸= Adjudication ̸=
Rulemaking. The first performs an action; the second decides
whether one concrete deviation is justified; the third changes
what future cases may be admitted without fresh adjudication.
Learning makes this separation technically necessary when-
ever an adjudicated case can enter persistent memory or policy
state and later influence a broader authorization decision with-
out an explicit rulemaking transition. The security boundary
is constitutional rather than application-specific: possession
of a lower power does not imply possession of the higher one.
10.2 Structural applicability, not universal prevalence
The formal security object is the transition from a valid case
exception to standing reusable authority. A deployment falls
within our intended class when it has the four structural prop-
erties in Section 3: defeasible norms, bounded case discretion,
persistent experience reuse, and a separation between adjudi-
cation and rulemaking authority. The learner implementation
is deliberately abstract—it may be neural, retrieval-based,
symbolic, or adversarial—so the confinement result does not
depend on a particular physical or software domain. Intermit-
tent root availability is one reason to delegate case discretion,
but it is not part of the exception-ratcheting definition.
Structural applicability should not be confused with empiri-
cal prevalence. Our theorem says what must remain confined
if such a system reuses successful exceptions; it does not
establish that every deployed learning agent, or every current
LLM agent, frequently exhibits exception ratcheting. That
measurement remains a separate empirical question, as stated
in Section 11.
10.3 Positive feedback and normalization
Operational learning sees many successes and comparatively
few catastrophic failures. For high-consequence systems, the
first strong negative example may be the irreversible event
the norm was meant to prevent. This asymmetry is why prior
success is poor authority evidence. NASA’s normalization-of-
deviance guidance makes the same point for human organi-
zations [8]; PRECEDENT GUARD translates it into a machine-
enforceable separation between outcome evidence and norma-
tive evidence.
10.4 What may be learned from an exception?
Almost everything operational: a better actuator sequence,
faster diagnosis, improved resource prediction, or a candi-
date reusable rule. The only restriction is on the transition
from learned representation to standing normative authority
(Theorem 3).
10.5 Precedent is useful
Our precedent is deliberately machine-checkable. A certified
precedent is not “past success therefore permission.” It is a
cached proof that a reusable sufficient condition is already
inside the root-authored discretion contract. It accelerates
recurring cases without creating new law.
10.6 Multiple norms and conflicts
See (T4) in Section 3. Ordered normative restraining bolts
provide one approach to prioritized compliance [7]. PRECE -
DENT GUARD is applied after conflict resolution selects a
candidate deviation, or the root envelope can be vector-valued;
our prototype and evaluation isolate one norm at a time to
study ratcheting in a controlled setting, leaving an empirical
multi-norm evaluation to future work.
11. Limitations
Formalizable discretion. PRECEDENT GUARD assumes the
root can express a conservative machine-checkable upper
bound on local discretion. Some moral or social judgments
cannot be captured faithfully. Such deployments may need
coarse tiers or remote authority when available.
Current evidence can be wrong. As stated explicitly
in (T1), the system prevents success history from becoming
unauthorized rulemaking authority; it does not solve sensor
compromise, false evidence, or semantic misclassification of
the current situation. This is a distinct threat from exception
ratcheting and requires a separate evidence-integrity mech-
anism (e.g., attested sensing, independent cross-checks) to
address.
Counterfactual models are incomplete. A challenger
proves necessity only relative to its declared model. Section 8
now quantifies how over-approximation trades off against con-
servative rejection; an unsound (under-approximating) model,
rather than merely conservative one, would undermine the
safety guarantee, and Assumption 5 requires soundness be
established separately for any deployedMc.
Structural generality is not an empirical prevalence
claim. The learner in every experiment in this paper is an
adversarial generalizer, not a trained model. This strengthens
the mechanism guarantee (it is a worst-case argument) and
shows that the confinement proof is not tied to one learner
implementation, but it does not measure how frequently, or un-
der what prompting/memory conditions, current LLM agents
actually exhibit exception-ratcheting-like generalization in
practice. An earlier internal discussion of this paper’s revi-
sions proposed adding a qualitative case study with a real
learning agent (e.g., comparing a history-reuse baseline, an
LLM-judge baseline, and PRECEDENT GUARD on a repeated-
success workload). We did not include that case study in this
version: producing it honestly requires actually instrumenting
and observing a real learning agent’s behavior over a repeated-
success workload, which is a measurement exercise distinct
from, and not derivable from, the formal and enumerative

12. Related Work
Normative multi-agent systems. Oren et al. formalize nor-
mative power [ 1]; Roshankish and Fornara formalize rich
norm types, exceptions, and powers [ 2]; Morris-Martin et
al. survey norm emergence and bottom-up formation [ 12].
PRECEDENT GUARD treats unauthorized bottom-up standing-
rule formation as a security violation when no rulemaking
power was delegated.
Runtime norm revision. Dell’Anna et al. use execution
data and agent preferences to revise sanctions at runtime [3, 4].
Their revision is explicit and intentional. Our problem is de
facto revision produced by ordinary learning when the learner
lacks normative revision authority.
Safe and normative learning. Restraining-bolt work
separates authority-specified temporal constraints from a
reinforcement-learning agent [5, 6]. Normative and ordered
variants target legal/social/ethical compliance and hierarchical
norm violation [ 7, 13]. Our typed precedent layer governs
how a defeasible exception may become reusable.
Reference monitoring and proof-carrying authority.
Least privilege and execution monitoring motivate moving
security decisions outside untrusted code [ 9, 10]. Proof-
carrying authentication requires evidence supporting autho-
rization [14]; proof-carrying agent actions extend this idea to
agent runtime governance [15]. PRECEDENT GUARD applies
proof-carrying design to normative generalization rather than
only current action authorization.
Normalization of deviance. NASA safety guidance warns
that repeated successful deviation can normalize unsafe prac-
tice and cautions against using past success to redefine accept-
able performance [8]. We translate that lesson into a formal
learning-system boundary: success may update competence
but not normative authority.
13. Conclusion
A learning system can be authorized to interpret a rule without
being authorized to rewrite it. Exception ratcheting appears
when that distinction is lost: a valid case succeeds, success
becomes learning signal, and the learned pattern is later treated
as standing authority even though no rulemaking transition
occurred.
PRECEDENT GUARD makes the missing separation explicit.
A CASE is one adjudication; a PRECEDENT is reusable author-
ity that must independently prove containment in the current
root envelope; an AMEND object is the only operation that
may widen that envelope. The confinement theorem is agnos-
tic to how aggressively the learner generalizes and to whether
the containment verifier is monotone or non-monotone. Oper-
ational learning may therefore become faster, richer, and more
reusable without silently acquiring constitutional power.
A case may teach the system. It may not legislate.
That is the boundary this paper enforces: autonomous discre-
tion without autonomous legislation.
Ethics Considerations
This work evaluates a synthetic security model, two
containment-verifier implementations, and local enumera-
tive/simulated experiments. It does not involve human sub-
jects, private datasets, deployed autonomous equipment, or
interaction with external systems.
We nonetheless note a dual-use consideration specific to
this mechanism. PRECEDENT GUARD is designed to prevent
operational success from becoming unauthorized normative
authority; it cannot make an unsafe root envelope safe, and it
cannot validate fraudulent context evidence (T1). A malicious
root, or a compromised evidence pipeline able to fabricate
thee that a CASE or PRECEDENT proof is checked against,
could use formally valid CASE/PRECEDENT objects to le-
gitimize discretion that should not have been granted. Those
attacks are explicitly outside our TCB assumptions (Section 3),
and we state them here rather than leaving the mechanism’s
authority guarantee to be mistaken for an evidence-integrity
or root-trustworthiness guarantee it does not provide.

A. Bounded Stress-Test Models
DSL-A. The root envelope used by the artifact isD(u,a,r ) =
min(4,⌊(u +a + 2r)/4⌋), withu,a∈ [0, 7] andr∈{ 0, 1}.
The initial reusable rule is u ≥ 6,a ≥ 6,r = 1,d ≤ 3.
Each success allows the generalizer to propose one of five
transitions: decrement urgency, decrement alternative scarcity,
drop reversibility, increment maximum deviation, or remain
unchanged. Since D is monotone, containment reduces to
evaluating the rule’s least-favorable point.
DSL-B. The root envelope is DB = ( smoke ∧
pressureStable∧ tox≤ 6)∨ (evac∧ backupVent∧ tox≤
8), with boolean evidence variables and tox ∈ [0, 10].
The initial reusable rule is the single clause smoke =
1, pressureStable = 1, tox≤ 3. Each success allows the gen-
eralizer to propose one of five transitions: raise the clause’s
toxicity bound by one, drop the smoke requirement, drop
the pressure-stable requirement, add a second clause exactly
matching the evacuation branch ( evac = 1, backupVent =
1, tox≤ 8), or remain unchanged. BecauseDB is not mono-
tone, containment is decided by exhaustive enumeration over
the full 176-point bounded evidence domain per clause, rather
than at a single least-favorable point.
Both the history-reuse baseline (installs every proposal un-
conditionally) and PRECEDENT GUARD (installs a proposal
only if it passes the respective containment check) are run
over all histories up to the stated length, exhaustively for both
DSLs.
B. Artifact and Reproducibility Notes
All numbers reported in Section 8 were produced by five self-
contained Python scripts (no network or GPU dependency):
(1) DSL-A sampling-sensitivity, (2) DSL-B exhaustive stress
test and sampling-sensitivity, (3) challenger conservatism
sweep over synthetic mission DAGs (25–55 nodes, fixed seed),
(4) bounded-challenger load simulation (single-queue-with-
budget model, service times drawn from a lognormal fit to
the measured Table 7 quantiles), and (5) an adversarial 20×
overload flood using the same queueing model. The ratchet-
ing experiments (DSL-A and DSL-B) are exhaustive for their
stated models; the DAG and load experiments use fixed pseu-
dorandom seeds. As discussed in Section 11, no experiment
in this artifact involves a trained or deployed LLM agent; all
“learners” are adversarial generalizers or, in the DoS simula-
tion, a synthetic Poisson/queueing request stream. The paper’s
security claims are conditional on the stated enforcement as-
sumptions, not on a measured likelihood of a specific model’s
behavior.
